## What it measures

Triglycerides are a fat that circulates in your blood. Calories your body does not need
straight away are converted into triglycerides and stored in fat cells, then released between
meals when energy is needed. Having some is normal and necessary.

They are measured as part of a lipid panel because a raised level is associated with heart
and blood vessel problems, and because triglycerides and cholesterol tend to move together.

## What high triglycerides means

A value above the range printed on your report means more of this fat is circulating than
that lab uses as its comparison point. Raised triglycerides are associated with a higher risk
of heart disease and stroke, and very high levels are also associated with inflammation of
the pancreas.

This is the number on the panel most easily disturbed by something temporary. A single raised
result in someone who did not fast, or who was recently unwell, often looks different when
repeated properly.

## What low triglycerides means

A value below the range is usually not a concern on its own. Low levels can occasionally be
linked to an overactive thyroid, malnutrition or a condition affecting how fat is absorbed.

## What can affect the result

Food is the big one. A recent meal raises triglycerides markedly, which is why many labs ask
you to fast for several hours before the blood is drawn — and why a non-fasting sample should
be read with that in mind. Alcohol in the days before the test, recent illness, pregnancy,
poorly controlled diabetes and some medicines all move the number too.

## Questions to ask your doctor

- Did I need to fast for this test, and did I?
- Should we repeat it as a fasting sample?
- What does this mean alongside my cholesterol results?
- Would changes to food, alcohol or activity be likely to make a difference for me?
