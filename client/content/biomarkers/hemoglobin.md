## What it measures

Hemoglobin is the iron-rich protein inside your red blood cells. It is what picks up oxygen
in your lungs and releases it to the rest of your body, and it is what makes blood red.

Measuring it says how much oxygen-carrying capacity your blood has. It is one of the most
frequently run tests there is, and it sits at the centre of a complete blood count alongside
hematocrit and the red cell count, which describe the same thing from different angles.

## What high hemoglobin means

A value above the range printed on your report can mean your body is making extra red blood
cells, which happens with some lung and heart conditions, with sleep apnea, and with a
condition called polycythemia vera.

It can also mean there is simply less fluid in your blood than usual, which concentrates
what is there. Dehydration is the common explanation, and it resolves on its own.

## What low hemoglobin means

A value below the range is called anemia. It has many causes: blood loss, too little iron,
vitamin B12 or folate, a long-term illness, a problem in the bone marrow, or an inherited
condition such as thalassemia.

Anemia is a finding, not a diagnosis. The useful question is what is causing it, which is
why your doctor will usually look at the red cell indices on the same report and may order
further tests.

## What can affect the result

Where you live matters: people at high altitude run higher hemoglobin because there is less
oxygen in the air. So do sex and age, which is why many labs print different ranges. Smoking
raises it. Pregnancy lowers it, because blood volume increases. Dehydration pushes it up and
a recent large drink of fluid can nudge it down. Some medicines affect it, and so does
having given blood recently.

## Questions to ask your doctor

- If this is low, do we know why, or do we need to look further?
- Could I be dehydrated, and should the test be repeated?
- Does the range on my report take my age and sex into account?
- How does this fit with the other red cell numbers on the page?
