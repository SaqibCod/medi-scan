## What it measures

Lymphocytes are white blood cells of two main kinds. B cells make antibodies against viruses,
bacteria and toxins. T cells recognise and destroy the body's own cells when those cells have
been infected by a virus or have become abnormal.

Between them they are the part of the immune system that remembers, which is why they are
central to recovering from a viral infection and to how vaccines work.

As with the other cell types, your report may give a percentage of all white cells or an
absolute count. A percentage moves when any other cell type moves, so it is worth knowing
which one you are looking at.

## What high lymphocytes means

A value above the range printed on your report is common during and after viral infections —
and after some of them the count stays raised for weeks once you feel perfectly well. Some
bacterial infections raise it too.

A persistently high count, particularly in an older adult with no infection to explain it,
is something a doctor will want to look into further.

## What low lymphocytes means

A value below the range can follow a recent infection, including a current one. Steroid
medicines lower it, as do chemotherapy and radiotherapy. Some autoimmune conditions and some
long-term infections lower it, and it can simply be low for a short while after illness or
physical stress.

## What can affect the result

Recent or current infection is the usual explanation in either direction. Steroids, stress,
chemotherapy and radiotherapy all lower it. The percentage figure also moves whenever
neutrophils move, without lymphocytes themselves having changed at all.

## Questions to ask your doctor

- Could a recent infection explain this?
- Is this a percentage or an absolute count?
- Should it be repeated once I have fully recovered?
- Does anything else on my report change how you read it?
