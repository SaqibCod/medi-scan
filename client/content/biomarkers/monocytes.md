## What it measures

Monocytes are the largest white blood cells. They kill bacteria, viruses and other germs,
they strengthen the wider immune response, and they clear away dead cells and debris
afterwards.

They are a small share of the white cell total, which has a practical consequence: the
percentage can look like it has moved a lot when the actual number has barely changed.

## What high monocytes means

A value above the range printed on your report is often seen during recovery from an
infection, when the clearing-up work is going on. Long-running infections, inflammatory
conditions and some autoimmune conditions raise it as well.

Much less commonly a persistently raised count reflects a condition of the blood or bone
marrow, which is why a doctor will want to see whether it settles.

## What low monocytes means

A value below the range is usually not treated as meaningful on its own, partly because the
normal numbers are small enough that ordinary variation can take it below a printed range.
A genuinely low count can occur with some bone marrow conditions and with medicines that
suppress the marrow.

## What can affect the result

Recent or recovering infection, inflammation, steroid medicines and chemotherapy all move it.
Because the counts are small, the percentage figure is easily shifted by changes in the much
more numerous neutrophils and lymphocytes rather than by anything happening to monocytes
themselves.

## Questions to ask your doctor

- Could a recent infection explain this?
- Is the absolute count outside the range, or only the percentage?
- Should it be repeated?
- Does it change anything, given the rest of my results?
