## What it measures

Neutrophils are the most common type of white blood cell and the body's main early defence
when bacteria or other germs get in. They arrive at the site of an infection first and in
large numbers.

A differential reports them either as a percentage of all white cells or as an absolute
count. The two can tell different stories: a percentage can rise simply because another cell
type fell, so check which one your report gives.

## What high neutrophils means

A value above the range printed on your report most often means the body is responding to a
bacterial infection or to inflammation somewhere. Recent injury, surgery, physical stress and
steroid medicines all raise neutrophils too, and so can smoking and pregnancy.

## What low neutrophils means

A value below the range is called neutropenia. It follows some viral infections, and it is
an expected effect of chemotherapy and some other medicines. It can also occur with
autoimmune conditions and with problems in the bone marrow.

Some healthy people, particularly of African or Middle Eastern ancestry, have counts that sit
naturally below a standard range without any associated problem. If the count is low enough
to matter, your doctor will say so and will usually explain what precautions, if any, apply.

## What can affect the result

Infection, inflammation, recent exercise, stress, smoking, pregnancy, steroids, and the
medicines used in chemotherapy. Because neutrophils respond quickly, this number can look
quite different a few days apart.

## Questions to ask your doctor

- Is my report giving a percentage or an absolute count?
- Could a recent infection or one of my medicines explain this?
- Should it be repeated, and when?
- Is this low enough that I need to take any precautions?
