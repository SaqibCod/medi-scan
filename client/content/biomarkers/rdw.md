## What it measures

RDW describes how much your red blood cells differ from one another in size. Healthy blood
contains cells of fairly uniform size; RDW puts a number on how uniform.

It is the companion to MCV. MCV gives the average size and RDW gives the spread, and the two
together say more than either alone — a normal average can hide a mixture of very small and
very large cells, and RDW is what reveals that.

## What high RDW means

A value above the range printed on your report means the cells vary in size more than that
lab's comparison point. This often happens when the body has recently started producing cells
differently — for example when a shortage of iron, vitamin B12 or folate is developing, or
beginning to be treated, so that older and newer cells differ.

A raised RDW has also been noted alongside a range of long-term conditions. On its own it is
a prompt to look further rather than a finding about any one of them.

## What low RDW means

A value below the range is not generally treated as meaningful. It says the cells are
unusually uniform in size, which is not a problem in itself.

## What can affect the result

A recent blood transfusion mixes in cells from someone else and raises RDW. So does recovery
from blood loss, and the early stages of treatment for anemia, because newly made cells
differ from the ones already circulating. Pregnancy can shift it.

## Questions to ask your doctor

- What does my RDW add to my MCV?
- Could a recent transfusion or recent treatment explain it?
- Should we check iron, B12 or folate?
- Does this need repeating?
