## What it measures

MCV is the average size of your red blood cells. It is one of the red cell indices, a small
group of numbers on a complete blood count that describe what the cells are like rather than
how many there are.

On its own it says little. Its value is in narrowing down the cause when another number —
usually hemoglobin — is low, because different causes of anemia tend to produce cells of
characteristically different sizes.

## What high MCV means

A value above the range printed on your report means your red cells are larger on average
than that lab's comparison point. Larger cells are classically associated with a shortage of
vitamin B12 or folate, and also turn up with liver conditions, an underactive thyroid, some
medicines including certain chemotherapy drugs, and regular alcohol use.

## What low MCV means

A value below the range means the cells are smaller on average. Smaller cells are classically
associated with too little iron, and with inherited conditions such as thalassemia. Long-term
illness can produce them too.

## What can affect the result

Because MCV is an average, a mixture can hide itself: someone short of both iron and B12 can
have small cells and large cells averaging out to a value inside the range. This is one
reason RDW sits beside it, since RDW describes how much the sizes vary rather than where
they centre.

Pregnancy, alcohol, some medicines and a recent blood transfusion all shift it.

## Questions to ask your doctor

- What does my MCV suggest about the cause of my result?
- Should we check iron, B12 or folate?
- How does it fit with my RDW?
- Should the test be repeated?
