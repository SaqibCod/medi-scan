## What it measures

MCHC describes how tightly packed the hemoglobin is inside your red blood cells — the amount
of hemoglobin relative to the size of the cell holding it.

Where MCH asks how much hemoglobin is in the average cell, MCHC asks how concentrated it is.
A small cell with a little hemoglobin can have a low MCH but a perfectly ordinary MCHC. The
lab calculates it from your hemoglobin and your hematocrit.

## What high MCHC means

A value above the range printed on your report is unusual, because there is a limit to how
much hemoglobin a cell can hold. It is seen in a condition called hereditary spherocytosis,
where the cells are smaller and rounder than normal.

More often a high MCHC points at the sample rather than the person: if red cells have broken
down in the tube, or the sample was cold or clumped, the calculation comes out high. Your
doctor may simply repeat it.

## What low MCHC means

A value below the range means the hemoglobin is less concentrated than that lab's comparison
point. The classic cause is too little iron. Thalassemia and some long-term illnesses do it
too, and it usually appears alongside a low MCV.

## What can affect the result

The same things that move the other red cell indices: iron, long-term illness, inherited
conditions. MCHC is also the index most sensitive to how the sample was collected and
handled, so an isolated odd result is worth repeating before reading much into it.

## Questions to ask your doctor

- Could this be something about the sample rather than about me?
- Should it be repeated?
- How does it fit with my MCV and MCH?
- Do we need to check my iron?
