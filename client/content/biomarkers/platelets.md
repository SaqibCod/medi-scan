## What it measures

Platelets are the smallest cells in your blood. When a vessel is injured they gather at the
site and stick together to form a plug, which is the first step in stopping bleeding.

A platelet count says how many there are. It does not say how well they work — that needs a
separate group of tests, which a doctor orders when someone bleeds or bruises easily despite
a normal count.

## What high platelets means

A value above the range printed on your report is called thrombocytosis. Most often it is
reactive: the count rises temporarily in response to infection, inflammation, recent surgery,
injury, or a shortage of iron. It settles once the cause does.

Less commonly the marrow is producing too many on its own account. Your doctor will usually
repeat the count before treating a single raised result as meaningful.

## What low platelets means

A value below the range is called thrombocytopenia. Causes include some infections, some
medicines, conditions where the immune system removes platelets, liver disease, and problems
in the bone marrow.

A mildly low count often causes no symptoms at all. Easy bruising, bleeding gums, or small
red spots on the skin are worth mentioning to your doctor promptly.

## What can affect the result

Platelets can clump together in the sample tube, which makes the counter read low when the
true count is normal — a common and harmless explanation for an unexpected result, and one
reason a repeat is often the next step. Pregnancy lowers the count slightly. Recent
infection, inflammation and some medicines move it in either direction.

## Questions to ask your doctor

- Could this be clumping in the sample rather than a real change?
- Should the count be repeated?
- Could any of my medicines be involved?
- What should I watch for in the meantime?
