## What it measures

Blood is part cells and part liquid. Hematocrit is the proportion that is red blood cells,
usually reported as a percentage.

Because it is a proportion, it moves for two quite different reasons: the number of red cells
can change, or the amount of liquid they are suspended in can change. Both show up the same
way on the report, which is why hematocrit is read alongside hemoglobin and the red cell
count rather than on its own.

## What high hematocrit means

A value above the range printed on your report can mean your body is producing more red blood
cells than usual — seen with some lung conditions, some heart conditions, and polycythemia.

Or it can mean there is less plasma than usual, so the same cells take up a larger share.
Dehydration is the most common reason for that, and it is also the easiest to correct.

## What low hematocrit means

A value below the range can mean recent blood loss, ongoing blood loss, or that fewer red
cells are being made than normal, which is anemia. It can also happen when another cell line
is overproduced, as in some bone marrow conditions.

In pregnancy a lower hematocrit is expected, because blood volume rises faster than red cell
production.

## What can affect the result

Age, sex, smoking and the altitude where you live all shift hematocrit, and labs set their
ranges accordingly. Dehydration raises it and extra fluid lowers it, so how much you had
drunk before the sample matters more here than for most tests. Pregnancy lowers it. Recent
blood donation lowers it.

## Questions to ask your doctor

- Does this reflect my red cells, or how hydrated I was?
- Should it be repeated at a different time?
- How does it fit with my hemoglobin and red cell count?
- Does the range on my report account for where I live?
