## What it measures

Red blood cells are made in your bone marrow and carry oxygen from your lungs to the rest of
your body. This test counts how many of them are in a measured volume of blood.

It is one of three closely related numbers on a complete blood count. Hemoglobin says how
much oxygen-carrying protein is present, hematocrit says what share of the blood is cells,
and this says how many cells there are. They usually move together, and where they do not,
the difference is itself informative.

## What high RBC means

A value above the range printed on your report can mean your body is making extra red cells.
That happens with conditions that leave the blood short of oxygen — some lung and heart
conditions, sleep apnea — and with polycythemia vera, where the marrow overproduces.

Living at high altitude raises it for the same reason. Dehydration raises it too, by
concentrating the blood rather than by adding cells.

## What low RBC means

A value below the range means fewer red cells than that lab's comparison point, which is one
of the ways anemia shows up. Causes include blood loss, too little iron or certain vitamins,
long-term kidney disease, and conditions affecting the bone marrow.

Pregnancy lowers it because blood volume rises.

## What can affect the result

Altitude, dehydration, pregnancy, recent blood donation, smoking, age and sex all move this
number, and most labs print different ranges for men and women. It is read together with the
red cell indices on the same report, which describe the size and hemoglobin content of the
cells and help point at a cause.

## Questions to ask your doctor

- Does this fit with my hemoglobin and hematocrit?
- If it is low, do we know the cause, or do we need more tests?
- Could dehydration or where I live explain it?
- Should the test be repeated?
