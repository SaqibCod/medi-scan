## What it measures

HDL stands for high-density lipoprotein. It is the particle that picks up cholesterol from
around the body and carries it back to the liver, where it can be broken down and removed.

That direction of travel is why it is often called "good" cholesterol, and it is why HDL
reads the opposite way to the rest of the panel: for most of these numbers a high result is
the one that gets flagged, and for HDL it is usually a low one.

## What high HDL means

A value above the range printed on your report is generally not treated as a problem, and
higher HDL has long been associated with lower cardiovascular risk. Very high values are
uncommon and your doctor may look at them alongside the rest of the panel.

Because HDL works in the opposite direction to LDL, some labs print its range as a minimum —
a "greater than" value — rather than as an upper limit. If the range on your report reads
that way, that is why.

## What low HDL means

A value below the range means less cholesterol is being carried back to the liver than that
lab uses as its comparison point. Low HDL is associated with a higher risk of heart disease,
and it is one of the things that makes a high total cholesterol more concerning rather than
less.

## What can affect the result

Smoking, being inactive and carrying extra weight are all associated with lower HDL, and
regular physical activity with higher HDL. Some medicines and some inherited conditions move
it too. Sex makes a difference, and many labs print different ranges for men and women for
this reason. A recent illness or pregnancy can shift the whole panel for a time.

## Questions to ask your doctor

- How should I read my HDL alongside my LDL and total cholesterol?
- Is my result something to act on, or to watch?
- Does my report use a different range for HDL because of my sex?
- Would more activity be likely to change this number for me?
