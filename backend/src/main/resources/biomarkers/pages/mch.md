## What it measures

MCH is the average amount of hemoglobin carried inside each red blood cell. The lab works it
out from your hemoglobin and your red cell count rather than measuring it directly.

Hemoglobin is what carries oxygen, so this describes how well loaded the average cell is.
It tends to track MCV closely, because bigger cells generally hold more hemoglobin, and the
two are usually read together.

## What high MCH means

A value above the range printed on your report means each cell carries more hemoglobin than
that lab's comparison point, which usually goes with cells that are larger than average. The
same causes apply as for a high MCV: a shortage of vitamin B12 or folate, liver conditions,
and some medicines.

## What low MCH means

A value below the range means the cells carry less hemoglobin than that lab's comparison
point, which usually goes with smaller cells. Too little iron is the most common reason, and
inherited conditions such as thalassemia also produce it.

## What can affect the result

Anything that changes the size of red cells tends to change MCH with it, so the influences
are much the same as for MCV: iron, vitamin B12 and folate, long-term illness, alcohol, some
medicines, and a recent transfusion.

Because it is calculated from two other numbers, anything that disturbs either of those — a
clotted or poorly handled sample, for instance — can show up here as well.

## Questions to ask your doctor

- Does my MCH add anything to what my MCV already shows?
- Should we check iron, B12 or folate?
- Is this worth repeating?
- How does it fit with my hemoglobin?
