## What it measures

Cholesterol is a waxy substance your body needs. It goes into the walls of your cells, into
some hormones, and into the vitamin D your skin makes. Your liver produces most of it, and
the rest comes from food.

Total cholesterol is the sum of all of it, whichever particle is carrying it. That makes it a
quick overall number, but a limited one: it adds together the kind linked to blocked arteries
and the kind that helps clear it away. This is why a lipid panel almost always breaks it down
into LDL, HDL and triglycerides as well.

## What high total cholesterol means

A result above the range printed on your report means more cholesterol is circulating than
that lab uses as its comparison point. Over many years, a high level is linked to fatty
deposits building up in the walls of blood vessels, which raises the risk of heart disease
and stroke.

The number on its own does not say much. A high total with a high HDL reads differently from
a high total with a low HDL, which is why your doctor looks at the whole panel together with
your other risk factors.

## What low total cholesterol means

A result below the range is usually not a concern by itself. Very low levels are less common
and can be linked to an overactive thyroid, liver conditions, malnutrition, or some
medicines. Your doctor can say whether it means anything in your case.

## What can affect the result

Diet, body weight, physical activity, smoking and alcohol all move cholesterol, and so do
things outside your control: age, family history, and for some people the menopause. Some labs
ask you to fast first, mainly because the triglycerides measured alongside it are sensitive
to a recent meal. Pregnancy and a recent serious illness can shift the whole panel for a
while.

Labs also differ in how they measure and calculate the parts of a lipid panel, which is one
reason their reference ranges are not identical.

## Questions to ask your doctor

- What does this number mean alongside my LDL, HDL and triglycerides?
- Does my family history or anything else change how you read it?
- Should the test be repeated, and after how long?
- Would changes to food or activity be likely to make a difference for me?
